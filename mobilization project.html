Mobilization and Regime change

Author

Alice MEYLAN, Juliana CAPITANACHI GAUTHY

Presenting our tentative research question and datasets

France has been seeing protests bloom for the past 3 months, first from high schoolers then students and finally these protests welcomed the gilets jaunes and demands about the cost of living.

Violence has been a central topic for these 3 months as both protesters and police officers have been accused of making excessive use of it. Most demands are vividly criticizing the government and people holding power. More than a million people have been in the streets protesting, some of them asking for a new kind of government. As it doesn’t seem to be coming, questions arise on the conditions that make for regime change and for protesters to be listened to.

Seeing the current situation in France and the fact that our master is pretty politicized, we have been wondering about different collective actions and their impact. This question also touches on violence and its impact on the response. To study that, there are multiple ways and variables we could look for and try to analyse.

Limiting our analyis to protests and mass mobilization is a deliberate choice although they are not the only ways to express discontentment. For example, analysing the results of boycott could be interesting, or petitions too. We could try to compare different collective action tools that are possible and that have been studied and/or used in the past. However, with the data we have selected for our analysis, our scope remains limited to protests and demonstrations, and mass mobilization.

Moreover, we also wish to put that in relation to the kind of regime these protests are opposing, are they democracies, autocracies, slipping from one to the other ? Is the regime using violence against the protesters ?

It would be interesting to study and see which regime is more likely to see protests emerge, and how they respond to the demands.

Finally, we could study the different end scenarios and even wonder which protests ended up with a regime change ? And how ?

In this project, we would like to try to answer the following question :

Does mass mobilization lead to political regime changes, and to what extent are these results impacted by the nature of the mobilization (violent or non-violent)?

Looking for datasets on this topic was interesting and we found that Harvard produced two on the subject, along with pdf files explaining their approach and variables.

The first on is called mass mobilization. In this data set, they focus on mobilization against standing powers, government. That way, they also include the response opposed to these mobilizations. Here, mass mobilization corresponds to at least 50 persons. They used four major newspapers to base their research and they made the choice to exclude protests stemming from foreign policies or religious community.

The second Harvard dataset is called NAVCO,for violent and non violent campaigns and outcome. This data set offers informations that differs from the mass mobilization set. It specifies more context on the campaign and an array of binary variables. It covers data starting from 1900. And they intend to focus on maximalist campaigns, looking to overthrow governments or expel foreign military,separatism, self determination.

Then, we turn to ACLED’s (Armed Conflict Location & Event Data) data set which simply measures the number of protests and violent demonstrations per country per year. It is an independent, impartial global monitor that collects, analyses, and maps data on conflicts and protest. We use their publicly available aggregated data entitled “Number of demonstration events by country by year”.

Finally, we have the Episode of regime transformation (ERT) data set. This data set identifies episodes of democratization and autocratization in V-Dem data sets. Data ranges from 1990 to 2025, including more than 680 episodes of regime transformations.The data contained in this data set will be key to determine which countries experienced regime changes and when these changes happened.

Merging all our datasets into one

Now that we have analysed each data sets individually, we must merge them into one clean dataset in order to facilitate our econometric analysis in the next part of the project. Ideally, our final dataset contains one row per country-year with the number of protests/demonstrations in that country-year, the ERT data for that country-year (political regime, changes…), and whether the protests/demonstrations in that country/year were more violent or non-violent.

This implies three key steps:

1 - Deciding which datasets / variables are useful.

2 - Matching the datasets in terms of time frame.

3 - Matching the datasets in terms of country coverage.

Clearly, the Mass Mobiilization dataset covers more countries over a larger time frame. Further, when looking into the codebook of both datasets, it appears that Mass Mobiilization is considerably more precise in its definition of protests:

  • They are defined as a gathering of 50 or more people making a demand for the government.

  • They must be aimed for the state or state policy.

  • It captures only national initiatives of protesting targeted at home policies.

  • It excludes protests for other motivations (eg. religious).

As opposed to the ACLED dataset, this gives us access to very precise and targeted data which perfectly fits our analysis objectives: whether politically-motivated protests lead to actual political changes. The period covered by Mass Mobilization is also signficantly larger.

Thus, our explanatory variable will be entirely based on the Mass Mobilization data contained in the mass_mob_cy data set.

2- To determine our period of analysis, we look at the dataset which starts the latest and the dataset which ends the earliest. In this way, we make sure that all our variables are available for the same period. The dataset which starts the latest is Mass Mobilization (mass_mob_cy) in 1990. The dataset which finishes the earliest is NAVCO dataset (navco_df) which finishes in 2019. Thus our time frame of analysis is 1990-2019.

First, let’s create a clean version of the NAVCO dataset (as for Mass Mobilization) containing only one row per country-year which determines whether, on average, the protests in each year were mainly violent or non-violent:

navco_cy <- navco_df |>
  summarise(
    n_violent    = sum(VIOL == 1, na.rm = TRUE),
    n_nonviolent = sum(NONVIOL == 1, na.rm = TRUE),
    .by = c(LOCATION, BYEAR)
  ) |>
  mutate(
    violent    = as.integer(n_violent >= n_nonviolent),
    nonviolent = as.integer(n_nonviolent >= n_violent)
  ) |>
  select(LOCATION, BYEAR, violent, nonviolent)

In this way, if a year was mainly violent it takes 1 for violent and 0 for nonviolent, and vice-versa. In the case of a mixed year (as much violence as not), both are equal to 1.

We restrict all our datasets to this time frame:
