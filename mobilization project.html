Presenting our tentative research question and datasets

France has been seeing protests blossom for the last 3 months, first from high schoolers then students and finally these protests welcomed the gilets jaunes and demands about the cost of living.

Violence has been a topic for these 3 months at protesters have been accused of making usage of it as well as police officers. Some demands are vividly critiquing the government and people holding power. More than a million people have been in the streets protesting, some of them asking for new kind of government. As it doesn’t seem to be coming, we are wondering on the conditions that make for regime change, for protesters being listened to.

Seeing the current situation in France and the fact that our master is pretty politicized, we have been wondering about different collective actions and their impact. This question also touches on violence and its impact on the response.To study that there are multiple ways and variables we could look for and try to analyse.

Stopping to protests and mass mobilization is a choice as they are not the only ways to express discontentment, analysing the results of boycott could be interesting as well, or petitions too. We could try to compare different collective action tools that are possible and that have been studied and/or used in the past.

Moreover, we also wish to put that in relation to the kind of regime these protests are opposing, are they democracies, autocracies, slipping from one to the other ? Is the regime using violence against the protesters ?

It would be interesting to study and see which regime is more likely to see protests emerge. And how they respond to the demands

Finally, we could study the different end scenarios ad even wonder which protests ended up with a regime change ? And how ?

In this project, we would like to try to answer the question :

Does mass mobilization lead to political regime changes, and to what extent are these results impacted by the nature of the mobilization (violent or non-violent)?

Looking for datasets on this topic was interesting and we found that Harvard produced two on the subject, along with pdf files explaining their approach and variables.

The first on is called mass mobilization. In this data set, they focus on mobilization against standing powers, government.That way, they also include the response opposed to these mobilizations. Here, mass mobilization corresponds to at least 50 persons. They used four major newspapers to base their research and they made the choice to exclude protests stemming from foreign policies or religious community.

The second Harvard dataset is called NAVCO,for violent and non violent campaigns and outcome. This data set offers informations that differ from the mass mobilization set. It specifies more context on the campaign and an array of binary variables. It covers data starting from 1900. And they intend to focus on maximalist campaigns, looking to overthrow governments or expel foreign military,separatism, self determination.

Then, we’re using ACLED’s data set

Finally, we have de Episode of regime transformation data set. This data set is truly filled to the brim with useful and precise info.

Dataset and variable description

The ert_dem dataset identifies episodes of democratization and autocratization in V-Dem datasets. Data ranges from 1990 to 2025, including more than 680 episodes of regime transformations.The data contained in this dataset will be key to determine which countries experienced regime changes and when these changes happened. With the ultimate objective being to determine whether mass mobilization leads to political regime changes, we select a series of variables from this dataset which will be useful for our analysis:

Variables Description Type
country_id V-Dem country identifier. double (integer, [3,236])
country_text_id V-Dem country identifier in text. character
country_name Full V-Dem country name. NULL
year Year of observation. double (integer, [1900,2025])
v2x_regime The regime type for the country-year according to Regimes of the World (4 values). double (0 - closed autocracy, 1 - electoral autocracy, 2 - electoral democracy, 3 - liberal democracy)
reg_type Classifies whether the regime in that country-year is a democracy or autocracy. double (0 - autocracy, 1 - democracy)
v2x_polyarchy Gives the country’s score on the Electoral Democracy Index (EDI, V-DEM) in that year. double (interval [0,1])
row_regch_event Measures whether there was a regime change in that country-year according to Regimes of the World (not necessarily “genuine” = followed by elections) double (-1 - change from democracy to autocracy, 0 - no regime change, 1 - change from autocracy to democracy)

Cleaning the dataset and variable modification:

The rest of the variables contained in the dataset are removed. This includes: episode description variables and outcome variables, the new dataset is called ert_df_clean:

ert_df_clean <- ert_df |>
  select(country_id, country_text_id, country_name, year,v2x_regime, reg_type, v2x_polyarchy, row_regch_event, reg_trans, reg_start_year, dem_ep, dem_ep_start_year, aut_ep, aut_ep_start_year)

Note that the large portion of NA for the dem_ep_start_year and aut_ep_start_year indicate “non applicable” observations, i.e. that there is no episode associated with this particular observation.

Further, as our goal is to determine whether mass mobilization leads to changes in political regimes. Thus, we must modify the dem_ep and aut_ep variables so that they indicate the year the democratization or autocratization episode begins, as opposed to every year it lasts. These variables are particularly interesting for out analysis as they capture the movements towards or away from democratization/autocratization, without necessarily implying a regime switch (which are more rare).

ert_df_clean <- ert_df_clean |>
  mutate(
    dem_ep_new = dem_ep == 1 & year == dem_ep_start_year,
    aut_ep_new = aut_ep == 1 & year == aut_ep_start_year
  )
